## Gameplay Loops (Draft)

In game design, a gameplay loop can be split into 3 main parts: a starting condition, a challenge, and an end goal.

In a game like&mdash;say&mdash;Counter-Strike, you start with some money to buy equipment, then you use that equipment to fight the opposing team, then one team succeeds in their goal while the other fails.

Regardless of who wins, the round ends and so does the gameplay loop.

But there's another layer to this.

The round ended, but the match hasn't yet; there's another gameplay loop on top.

The match ends only when one team wins 13 rounds.

Let's call each round a **micro-loop** while the entire match is the **macro-loop**.

### Other examples

Let's have other examples.

In Plants vs Zombies, a micro-loop ends when the level ends, and the macro-loop ends when Zomboss is defeated.

Same as before, and same for all these other games.

### Osu

How about Osu?

You can win a level, you could get a perfect grade, you could even top the leaderbaord, but the game doesn't really "end".

In this case, there is only one loop.

Once you finish the level, that's it.

The loop ends there; you can choose to start another, or not.

### Minecraft

Lastly, how about Minecraft?

The loop ends when you defeat the Ender Dragon right?

Well, many have never even really played survival, let alone kill the dragon, and yet they've definitely played Minecraft, and a game isn't a game unless there's a gameplay loop, so what's the loop?

... There is none.

Minecraft isn't exactly a game, but rather a toy.

Mini-games like Bedwars, Spleef, and Cops & Robbers do define a starting condition, a challenge, and an end goal, which Minecraft itself does not provide.

In that sense, Minecraft is merely a platform for mini-games.

### Where does ZZZ sit?

Right, so where does Zenless sit in all this?

On one hand, we have the super linear multiplayer and story-focused games where the gameplay loop is clearly defined.

Zenless has a pretty linear story, so it should belong here, right?

On the other hand, we have what's basically a game *platform* in which *mini*-games are built on top of.

Does Zenless fit in this too?

Yes, actually. And it's brought by the question from earlier: When does Zenless end?

## ZZZ as a platform (Draft)

We do know that the game doesn't end when you complete all the levels of any particular mode.

The only way I can see people considering the game finished is when the main story concludes, and I'm certain they're aware of it, but more on that later.

That said, I think the game&mdash;as it currently stands today&mdash;does have a complete macro gameplay loop wherein modes like Combat Simulation and Shiyu Defense reside in.

And since completing those modes doesn't mean you've completed the game, we can treat them the same way as mini-games in Minecraft.

These even include time-limited events.

But more importantly, even these contribute your progression in the macro loop.

## Core Thesis

So, what's my point?

Zenless is a gacha game, not a hack-and-slash, because the overarching gameplay loop is not centered on overcoming a challenge, it's when you're lucky enough to get the character that you want.

Then, you build *that* character so that it can contribute to further improving your effectiveness in more difficult levels that reward more polychromes, which you need to&mdash;well&mdash;get more characters, faster!



And, I want to emphasize when I say "faster" here, because I think people overlook the reason why ZZZ is primarily identified as a gacha game rather than a hack-and-slash. For that matter, this applies to all games in the genre.

Normally, gameplay loops are designed to take maybe a few minutes to a few hours at most&mdash;maybe an entire day for more extreme sessions.

Gacha games' gameplay loops scale in the matter of days to **weeks**, *except* if you spend real-world money to buy your way into it.

By this definition, gacha games literally allow what's essentially the most direct way its players can pay to win.

And I don't mean that lightly. If you compare the progression tree between a free-to-play and someone who has a credit card, you'd be rioting with how unfair this is.



But, of course, I can see some arguments against this, so I'll tackle those too.

"Well, your argument is fundamentally flawed, because the core gameplay loop is actually X and not Y."

Okay, when you win in PUBG, you get this splash screen that reads "Winner Winner Chicken Dinner" and you go woohoo yay I won. When you defeat the Ender Dragon in Minecraft and you enter the end portal, the credits roll, you feel accomplished, and you get a bunch of XP and the dragon egg as a reward. When you finish a chapter in the main story in Zenless, you get *this*. When you finish a very difficult level against a boss, you get *the same thing*. And in both of them, do you *really* feel accomplished, or is it more like you finished part of your routine?

(On both of them: a standard pop-up showing what rewards you got.)

Critically, when you get an S-tier character on a banner you really really want... (show epic character screen showing how hot and sexy they are) only then do you actually feel any form of satisfaction. If **that** isn't definitive proof that the game centers around winning the character that you want, I don't know what is.



"Well, for pay-to-win players, they're kind of ruining the fun for themselves."

Are they, though? What I want to question is not whether *they* had fun entering their credit card number, but rather if *you* had fun grinding, doing some fun things, some that you only *kinda* enjoy, some that you don't like *at all*.

There is a mode in Zenless called "Periodic Conquest"; basically, an extraction game where you collect loot, do things with loot, bring some of said loot to improve performance, go in more dangerous areas, rinse and repeat.

I do not personally enjoy this game mode. I don't want to have to learn yet another entire ass game's worth of gameplay&mdash;gameplay that I don't really enjoy&mdash;just for more polychromes. And that's only **one** of the *many* game modes this game offers.

On one hand, sure, more options, just ignore the ones you don't like! Can you, though? If you're under pressure from the obvious FOMO mechanic this entire genre is build around, and especially with how stingy this game is with its polychrome distribution, if you want that character badly enough, you will very likely play through modes you don't enjoy at all just so you get that dopamine hit from finally having that barely-clothed mommy.

## The game I wanted to love

As I said in the intro, I didn't use Zenless as an example because I hate this game specifically, but rather because it's the one I wanted to like the most.

See, Zenless is not my first gacha game; that would be Genshin Impact, a game I have since left for many reasons, much of which I'll discuss in another video. Zenless isn't even the second, as I've also played *and left* Honkai: Star Rail, Azur Lane, and Blue Archive.

I had good hopes for Zenless, seeing how well-done the animations are in their trailers and how striking the game's visual theme is&mdash;I had hoped they found the problems that ultimately made me leave Genshin behind, and thought over the entire structure from the ground up to improve player experience.

I even had my first playthrough recorded because I wanted to make a review of this game right when it came out, y'know, from a game developer's perspective.

And, to be fair, there were definitely improvements, but I'm not convinced they *want* to fix the overly verbose dialogue or the "so hand-holdy that it's useless" tutorials or the myriad of other problems&mdash;all things I noticed on my very first playthrough up until today.

Despite all that, well, I already have well over a hundred hours on Steam alone, and dammit, I'm gonna keep playing it.
