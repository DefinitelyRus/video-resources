## Intro

10 years ago, if you were to search something on, say, Google, you would get results that more or less matched your search terms. The better you are at using the right terms, the more accurate results you would get.

Today, the same systems still exist: you input text and you get relevant results, but not as relevant as a decade ago. If you’re old enough, you might have noticed this degradation in search quality, not just from Google, but from all search engines.

Shouldn’t search get better after a decade of technological advancements, not worse?! Where did it all go so wrong?

## The Early Internet

It all begins here, and here, and here—with the early internet. Everyday people would build their own websites, link their pages together, and create communities of interconnected hubs made by mere individuals who simply liked what they were doing.

Sure, these websites were often buggy, insecure, or outright inaccessible because the owner didn’t set something up correctly, but that didn’t stop people from exploring them or building their own.

## MySpace

<!-- shorten this -->

And then there came MySpace.

Before MySpace, if you wanted your own corner of the internet, you had to learn basic web hosting and HTML. MySpace, however, vastly simplified this by allowing users to use templates that anyone could claim, all while preserving the individual expression and customization.

You could customize backgrounds, change layouts, embed music, curate your top 8 friends, and even inject raw code directly into your profile. It was the heyday for individual expression in the online world.

But that absolute freedom was also its greatest weakness. The platform became too bloated and difficult to navigate, profiles took minutes to load, music players crashed browsers, and most critically of all, malicious scripts could easily affect any visitor’s computer.

The platform grew and people loved it, but its failure to properly support the weight of its own promised freedom caused the website’s own demise.

## Beginning of the Modern Internet

All the while, Facebook swooped right in where MySpace failed to deliver. It was sterile and limited, but it was stable, secure, and highly available.

It spread like wildfire. People told their friends, who told their family, who told their dogs to make Facebook accounts, all of whom appear to have listened. There, they posted posts, chatted chats, and grouped into groups for whatever the hell they pleased. And, **if** they wish for it to, anyone else outside of Facebook could find their posts and profiles by use of a standard search engine.

### Popularization of Algorithms

Fast forward to the early 2010s. Having recognized that they had a massive, locked-in userbase, Facebook’s focus began to shift. Gone was the chronological feed from your friends and groups. Long live algorithmic content delivery.

While it felt nothing more than slightly frustrating at the time to see posts from others you never followed, people quickly got used to it, not that many of them bothered to manage their follow list to begin with.

With that, the shift fundamentally changed how information moved. Facebook effectively turned its platform into an internal search engine—one that proactively “searches” for you and serves you relevant content before you even ask.

### Effects of Algorithms

By keeping you trapped in an endless algorithmically-driven scroll, Facebook no longer needs the rest of the internet. It has you who consumes and engages with the content, and the people who create said content for tens, thousands, or even millions of people to see! Facebook doesn’t need to appeal to external search engines when their own users **effectively** and **unknowingly** force their friends and family to use it as a common point of contact! That’s 8 for the price of 1!

Suddenly, a massive chunk of human conversation, advice, and community interaction is entirely locked up behind Facebook’s walled garden. If you search \<A NICHE TOPIC HERE\>, you’re probably not gonna get a Facebook link. It’s not like people don’t care, it’s that people’s voices can’t be heard beyond the walls.

Don’t get me wrong, it’s still entirely possible to get Facebook results in your searches, but it probably won’t be the first, and **certainly** only those made externally available. With that said, it’s not the worst offender yet.

### It’s still fine out there

Looking more broadly, there’s still a wealth of information available from hundreds of thousands of websites. You could even argue that perhaps it’s for the best that Facebook is mostly kept out of the information pool given its… reputation. So, if you needed information about something, you probably found yourself in a blog, online libraries, public documentation, or community forums.

Many people were fine with this structure, myself included. The internet got smaller and more sterile, sure, but I’m no longer risking my banking details on sketchy websites some stranger made halfway across the globe.

## Discord

Discord.

Discord in itself was not a problem. At its core, it’s just another messaging platform structured such that it bridges the gap between a group chat and a forum. It provided high-quality low-latency voice calls and video streaming for free, and gamers around the world flocked to it. Even businesses envied them, having been stuck with poor quality or ludicrously expensive communication platforms—that’s how good of a tool it is.

But you and I didn’t realize that its greatness, once again, was making the internet even smaller. For one reason or another, people began preferring to spend their time tucked away inside private Discord servers where, inevitably, mountains of genuinely useful, interesting, and hyper-niche information were shared—completely, utterly, and permanently inaccessible to the rest of the internet.

### Just how bad is it?

To emphasize just how locked out we’ve become, you can’t just type into a search bar. Nooooo. Now, you must:

Known that the info exists inside a Discord server in the first place

Have a Discord account

- Know exactly which server it’s in
- Have an active, unexpired invite link to it
- Have specific role access to the exact channel the info is in
- **And read through 37 completely unrelated messages BECAUSE PEOPLE CAN’T STOP TALKING OVER EACH OTHER**

Imagine for a second that Google couldn’t show you how to beat a boss in Zelda unless you physically walked inside a building, shook hands with the owner, signed up for their private club, and were escorted to a hidden back room. **That is what is happening with Discord!**

### Forums vs Discord

Look, the problem isn’t that the information doesn't exist anywhere else outside of Discord. It most likely does, and probably in a much better, cleaner format. The issue isn’t existence; it’s surface area. I am certain you’ve had that one niche tech issue that only some ancient Russian guide from 2008 has ever solved. Perhaps if that new information wasn’t trapped inside a closed Discord channel, we’d actually have a modern English guide for it by now!

Sure, old internet forums are sometimes—okay, often—full of outdated information or plagued with toxic users. But at least the data is there. At least it's indexed. At least I can choose to go somewhere else and avoid the toxicity. And perhaps that's exactly what people felt when they decided to abandon forums and ask their questions on Discord instead.

So, in effect, Discord ate a slice of the pie forums were supposed to own, and that’s not even the worst to come for forum sites.
